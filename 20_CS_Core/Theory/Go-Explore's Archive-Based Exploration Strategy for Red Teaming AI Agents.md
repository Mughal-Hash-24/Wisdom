# Go-Explore's Archive-Based Exploration Strategy for Red Teaming AI Agents

Go-Explore’s archive-based exploration strategy redefines adversarial testing for AI agents by systematically revisiting and leveraging promising states to expose vulnerabilities. This section dissects its mechanics, from storage and retrieval of critical states to integration with red teaming workflows, while evaluating empirical outcomes and unresolved challenges.

## Introduction to Go-Explore and Red Teaming AI Agents

Go-Explore introduces an archive-based exploration paradigm that systematically revisits and expands promising states to overcome the limitations of traditional methods in reinforcement learning and adversarial testing. This section examines Go-Explore’s core mechanics, its motivations for red teaming AI agents, and how it surpasses conventional exploration strategies.

### Overview of Go-Explore as an Exploration Strategy

Go-Explore functions as a reinforcement learning exploration strategy designed to mitigate premature convergence by systematically revisiting and expanding promising states in complex environments. Its core mechanism relies on two primary components: **archive-based state storage** and **novelty-driven policy updates**. The archive maintains a repository of high-reward or otherwise significant states encountered during exploration, enabling the algorithm to return to these states and expand them further rather than fixating on suboptimal local optima. This contrasts with traditional exploration methods that often dissipate effort across low-reward regions due to undirected randomness or gradient-based myopia.

The strategy’s relevance to reinforcement learning emerges from its ability to decouple exploration from exploitation by explicitly prioritizing states with latent potential for discovery. By storing and revisiting states, Go-Explore ensures that exploration remains goal-agnostic yet systematically deepens coverage of high-value regions. This is particularly critical in environments where reward signals are sparse or deceptive, as it prevents the agent from abandoning promising trajectories prematurely. The archive’s role extends beyond mere storage; it serves as a dynamic map of the state space, guiding the agent toward regions that warrant further investigation based on intrinsic novelty metrics or extrinsic rewards.

In decision-making contexts, Go-Explore’s approach aligns with the need for robust exploration in domains where partial observability or stochastic transitions obscure optimal paths. The strategy’s emphasis on revisiting and expanding states mirrors human problem-solving behaviors in open-ended tasks, where iterative refinement of known solutions often yields breakthroughs that purely exploratory methods miss. By leveraging the archive as a prioritized frontier, Go-Explore transforms exploration from a stochastic gamble into a structured, cumulative process.

### Key Motivations for Go-Explore in AI Red Teaming

Red teaming AI agents exposes critical flaws in safety, robustness, and alignment by adversarially probing systems to uncover vulnerabilities before deployment. Traditional exploration strategies—such as random search, evolutionary methods, or gradient-based optimization—struggle in this domain because they rely on dense reward signals or smooth gradient landscapes, which are absent in adversarial settings. In red teaming, reward signals are often sparse, delayed, or entirely absent until an agent stumbles upon a catastrophic failure mode, making it difficult to guide exploration effectively. Even when rewards exist, they frequently lead to local optima where agents exploit superficial patterns rather than discovering deeper, more dangerous failure modes. For example, an agent might repeatedly trigger a benign edge case (e.g., misclassifying a slightly rotated image) while overlooking subtle adversarial perturbations that induce harmful behavior, such as bypassing safety filters or generating toxic outputs under specific contextual triggers.

Go-Explore addresses these challenges by decoupling exploration from exploitation through an archive-based strategy that explicitly prioritizes novelty and long-term memory retention. The core mechanism involves maintaining an archive of visited states, where each state is evaluated not just for immediate reward but for its potential to unlock new regions of the state space. This contrasts with traditional methods that either discard or undervalue historical data, leading to redundant exploration. By re-visiting and expanding upon promising states—even those with low immediate reward—Go-Explore systematically explores sparse reward landscapes without prematurely converging on suboptimal solutions. This is particularly critical in red teaming, where adversarial behaviors often emerge from rare, high-dimensional state interactions that standard exploration methods miss entirely.

The strategy also mitigates the risk of local optima by treating exploration as a multi-objective optimization problem. Instead of optimizing for a single reward signal, Go-Explore balances novelty, diversity, and adversarial potential, ensuring that agents do not fixate on easily exploitable but ultimately shallow failure modes. For instance, in language model red teaming, Go-Explore can prioritize states where the model generates outputs that are both novel (unseen in the archive) and adversarially effective (e.g., bypassing content filters), rather than settling for repetitive or easily detectable toxic outputs. This systematic uncovering of adversarial behaviors is where Go-Explore fills a critical gap left by traditional methods, which often fail to explore beyond the "low-hanging fruit" of obvious vulnerabilities.

### Comparison with Traditional Exploration Methods

Traditional exploration methods in reinforcement learning and AI red teaming rely on stochastic or heuristic-driven strategies that prioritize short-term gains or probabilistic novelty, often at the expense of systematic coverage. Random search, for instance, samples actions uniformly without memory, making it blind to previously visited states and prone to redundant traversals. Epsilon-greedy policies balance exploration and exploitation by occasionally selecting random actions, but their undirected nature fails to accumulate knowledge about high-reward or high-risk regions, leading to inefficient convergence. Intrinsic motivation methods, such as those using prediction errors or curiosity-driven rewards, incentivize agents to explore novel states, yet they often conflate novelty with utility, diverting focus toward irrelevant or trivial variations rather than meaningful adversarial scenarios.

Go-Explore’s archive-based strategy fundamentally diverges from these approaches by decoupling exploration into two phases: *archive growth* and *robustification*. The archive stores high-reward or high-coverage states as stepping stones, ensuring no previously discovered path is lost. Unlike random search, which discards prior states, or intrinsic motivation, which may chase ephemeral novelty, Go-Explore treats the archive as a persistent map of the state space, enabling targeted revisits to critical regions. During robustification, the agent retraces these stored paths while applying perturbations to uncover latent vulnerabilities, a process that classical methods cannot replicate without explicit state retention. This dual-phase design guarantees both breadth and depth, addressing the myopic tendencies of epsilon-greedy policies and the undirected exploration of intrinsic rewards. By leveraging the archive as a structured substrate, Go-Explore transforms exploration from a chaotic search into a deliberate, iterative refinement of adversarial strategies.

## Core Principles of Archive-Based Exploration

Archive-based exploration in Go-Explore redefines how agents revisit, prioritize, and exploit historical states to guide systematic discovery, replacing ad-hoc sampling with structured memory-driven search. This section dissects the core principles underpinning the archive’s role, its operational mechanics as a search strategy, and the theoretical foundations that decouple exploration from immediate rewards.

### Definition and Role of Archives in Exploration

In Go-Explore, an archive functions as a structured repository that stores and organizes explored states of an environment, enabling systematic revisitation and comparison. Unlike traditional exploration methods that discard or ignore previously encountered states, the archive preserves them as first-class entities, allowing agents to leverage historical data for future decisions. Each entry in the archive captures the state’s configuration, the path taken to reach it, and metadata such as novelty scores or performance metrics, which are critical for guiding exploration.

The archive’s role extends beyond mere storage. It serves as a dynamic map of the environment, where states are not just recorded but evaluated for their potential to unlock new regions or refine existing knowledge. Traditional methods often rely on stochastic or gradient-based search, which can overlook promising but distant states. The archive mitigates this by prioritizing revisits to states with high utility, such as those that lead to uncharted areas or exhibit unusual transitions. This prioritization is typically governed by a scoring function that balances novelty, distance, and relevance to the agent’s objectives.

Archives differ fundamentally from conventional exploration strategies by inverting the relationship between exploration and exploitation. Instead of treating exploration as a transient phase, the archive embeds it into the agent’s long-term memory, enabling persistent and adaptive revisitation. For example, in sparse-reward environments, the archive ensures that states with latent potential—such as those near bottlenecks or critical transitions—are not abandoned after a single visit. The archive also facilitates hierarchical exploration, where low-level details (e.g., pixel observations) are abstracted into higher-level states, reducing the search space and improving efficiency.

### Archive-Based Exploration as a Search Strategy

Archive-based exploration reframes search as a structured traversal of a state space where archives act as both memory and guide. Unlike random exploration, which samples actions uniformly without regard for prior outcomes, or greedy exploration, which fixates on immediate rewards while ignoring long-term diversity, archive-based strategies leverage stored trajectories to bias future actions toward unexplored yet promising regions. Archives maintain a non-redundant set of states or trajectories, each annotated with metrics such as novelty, reward, or visitation frequency, enabling the agent to prioritize revisits to high-potential or underexplored areas rather than retracing suboptimal or already-mastered paths.

The archive functions as a dynamic priority queue where selection is guided by intrinsic objectives like maximizing state novelty or extrinsic rewards accumulated along stored trajectories. When an agent encounters a state, it queries the archive for similar states using distance metrics in state space, retrieving the most relevant entries to inform action selection. This retrieval process avoids the inefficiency of random restarts by anchoring exploration in previously validated transitions, effectively pruning the search tree of dead ends and redundant branches. By coupling archive queries with policy rollouts, the agent can extrapolate from known successful trajectories, extending exploration along promising manifolds rather than dispersing effort across the entire state space.

Critically, the archive’s structure enforces a form of curriculum learning: states are added or updated based on their contribution to coverage or performance, ensuring that the agent’s focus shifts from broad, shallow sampling to targeted, deep dives into regions that either remain novel or yield high returns. This mechanism distinguishes archive-based exploration from undirected methods by embedding historical knowledge directly into the search process, transforming exploration from a stochastic process into a guided, evidence-driven strategy.

### Key Components of Archive-Based Exploration

**The Archive as a Structured Memory of Exploration**

The archive in Go-Explore’s strategy is not a passive storage mechanism but an active, hierarchical repository that organizes visited states by their novelty and potential for further discovery. Each entry in the archive represents a state—defined by the agent’s position, environment configuration, and internal variables—paired with metadata that quantifies its utility for exploration. The archive’s structure typically employs a grid or k-d tree to partition the state space, enabling efficient nearest-neighbor queries for state selection and revisitation. Novelty scores, often derived from intrinsic motivation metrics like prediction error or state visitation frequency, determine how states are prioritized and stored. High-novelty states are retained as "frontier" nodes, while low-novelty states are either discarded or deprioritized, ensuring the archive remains focused on unexplored or underexplored regions. This selective retention prevents the archive from bloating with redundant data while preserving states that could serve as stepping stones for future exploration.

**State Selection Criteria: Balancing Novelty and Potential**

State selection in archive-based exploration relies on a multi-objective criterion that evaluates both the novelty of a state and its potential to lead to new discoveries. Novelty is quantified using intrinsic rewards, such as the prediction error of a forward dynamics model trained to anticipate the next state given the current state and action. States with high prediction error are deemed novel because the model fails to explain their transitions, indicating uncharted territory. However, novelty alone is insufficient; the selection process also prioritizes states with high "potential," which is often estimated by the number of unvisited neighboring states or the diversity of actions that can be taken from the state. This dual criterion ensures that the agent does not get trapped in regions of high novelty that lack further exploration pathways. For example, in sparse-reward environments like Montezuma’s Revenge, states with high novelty but low potential (e.g., isolated rooms with no exits) are deprioritized in favor of states that act as hubs to multiple unvisited areas.

**Revisitation Mechanisms: Path Reconstruction and Policy Transfer**

Revisitation mechanisms enable the agent to return to promising states in the archive, either to refine its understanding of those states or to use them as starting points for further exploration. The most common approach is path reconstruction, where the agent uses a stored trajectory (sequence of states and actions) to navigate back to a target state. This requires maintaining not just the states themselves but also the actions taken to reach them, often stored as part of the archive’s metadata. When revisiting a state, the agent may also transfer a pre-learned policy or skill that was effective in that state’s neighborhood, accelerating exploration in similar regions. For instance, if the agent revisits a state where a specific sequence of actions reliably opens a door, it can reuse that skill in nearby states where the door configuration is similar. This policy transfer reduces the need for exhaustive retraining and leverages past successes to guide future actions. Additionally, revisitation can trigger fine-tuning of the archive’s novelty metrics, as revisited states may reveal new dynamics or hidden transitions that were not apparent during the initial visit.

**Interaction Dynamics: A Feedback Loop of Exploration and Refinement**

The three components—archive, state selection, and revisitation—form a feedback loop that drives continuous exploration. The archive’s novelty scoring informs state selection, which in turn determines which states are revisited. Revisitation refines the agent’s policies and updates the archive’s metadata, which can alter future novelty scores and selection criteria. For example, revisiting a state to test a new action may reveal that the state’s novelty score was artificially inflated due to a temporary environmental change (e.g., a flickering pixel in a video game). The archive’s metadata is updated to reflect this, and the state’s priority is adjusted accordingly. This dynamic interplay ensures that the exploration strategy remains adaptive, with the archive acting as both a compass and a repository of learned knowledge. The result is a self-correcting system where exploration is guided by past discoveries, and past discoveries are continually reevaluated in light of new evidence.

### Advantages of Archive-Based Exploration

Archive-based exploration fundamentally transforms how red teaming agents navigate complex action spaces by decoupling the search process from immediate reward signals. The archive acts as a persistent memory of visited states, enabling systematic rediscovery and reuse of promising trajectories without redundant exploration. This eliminates the inefficiency of traditional methods that treat each episode as an isolated trial, where agents must relearn or stumble upon useful behaviors repeatedly. By storing and retrieving high-reward or novel states, the archive accelerates convergence toward optimal policies, particularly in sparse-reward environments where traditional reinforcement learning struggles to propagate meaningful feedback.

The strategy’s primary advantage lies in its ability to handle multi-step scenarios by decomposing them into reusable sub-trajectories. Traditional exploration methods, such as random search or intrinsic motivation, often fail to preserve intermediate progress, forcing agents to rediscover critical transitions (e.g., unlocking doors, bypassing authentication gates) in every new episode. The archive mitigates this by retaining states that represent partial solutions, allowing agents to chain these fragments into longer, coherent attack sequences. For instance, an agent that discovers a privilege escalation path in one run can later retrieve and extend that path in subsequent runs, even if the environment resets or varies slightly.

Efficiency gains are further amplified in high-dimensional spaces where the curse of dimensionality renders exhaustive search intractable. Archive-based methods prioritize states based on novelty or reward potential, focusing computation on unexplored regions rather than wasting steps on previously visited configurations. This targeted exploration contrasts with traditional methods like epsilon-greedy policies or entropy maximization, which often waste resources on unproductive actions or get trapped in local optima. The archive’s selection mechanisms (e.g., curiosity-driven novelty search or performance-based prioritization) ensure that exploration remains adaptive, dynamically shifting focus as the agent uncovers new vulnerabilities or bypasses defenses.

Another critical benefit is the method’s robustness to environment stochasticity or partial observability. Traditional approaches frequently rely on fixed exploration schedules or heuristic-driven exploration bonuses, which can break down when environments introduce noise or hidden state. The archive, however, maintains a history of states that have proven useful under varying conditions, allowing agents to generalize across perturbations. For example, an agent that exploits a race condition in one configuration can store the vulnerable state and reuse it even if the timing or system load changes in later episodes.

Finally, archive-based exploration enables hierarchical reasoning by allowing agents to treat the archive as a substrate for meta-learning. States in the archive can be clustered or abstracted into higher-level strategies, which agents can then combine or refine. This stands in contrast to traditional methods that operate at a fixed granularity, unable to leverage past discoveries for higher-order planning. The archive thus serves as both a tool for immediate efficiency and a foundation for long-term adaptation, making it uniquely suited for red teaming tasks that demand both depth and breadth of coverage.

### Theoretical Underpinnings: Exploration vs. Exploitation

The tension between exploration and exploitation in reinforcement learning and autonomous agent design is fundamentally a problem of information acquisition versus immediate reward maximization. Exploration seeks to discover novel states, actions, or policies that expand the agent’s operational envelope, while exploitation leverages known high-reward behaviors to optimize short-term performance. Archive-based exploration strategies resolve this tension by decoupling the discovery of new behaviors from their immediate utility, using an external archive to store and retrieve diverse trajectories or policies. The archive acts as a persistent memory of exploration, enabling agents to revisit and refine behaviors without conflating novelty with reward.

In Go-Explore’s framework, the archive’s role is not merely to accumulate data but to structure it into a navigable space where exploration and exploitation can be balanced through explicit selection criteria. The algorithm prioritizes cells—discretized regions of the state space—based on a scoring function that combines novelty, estimated potential for future reward, and distance from previously visited states. This scoring mechanism introduces a controlled bias toward unexplored regions while ensuring that high-value behaviors are not prematurely discarded. The archive’s density-based selection (e.g., selecting cells with the fewest prior visits) enforces exploration, while the retrieval of high-scoring trajectories for policy improvement drives exploitation. By separating the storage of diverse behaviors from their evaluation, the archive mitigates the myopia of traditional exploration methods like ε-greedy or Boltzmann policies, which often conflate exploration with randomness or suboptimal early rewards.

Theoretically, this separation aligns with the principle of *optimism in the face of uncertainty*, where the archive’s selection criteria implicitly assign higher value to unexplored regions, encouraging the agent to treat uncertainty as an opportunity rather than a risk. However, unlike methods that rely solely on intrinsic motivation (e.g., curiosity-driven exploration via prediction error), archive-based approaches ground exploration in the structural properties of the state space. The archive’s organization—whether via cell-based partitioning, k-nearest neighbors, or graph-based connectivity—defines the granularity of exploration and the trade-off between breadth and depth. For instance, coarse partitioning may prioritize global diversity but risk overlooking local optima, while fine-grained partitioning may enable precise exploitation but at the cost of computational overhead.

Critically, the archive’s ability to mediate exploration-exploitation trade-offs depends on its update dynamics. Go-Explore’s *stagnation detection* and *restart mechanisms* ensure that the archive does not become a static repository of outdated behaviors. When a cell’s performance plateaus, the agent resets to a prior state within that cell or a neighboring one, effectively re-engaging exploration without discarding the archive’s accumulated knowledge. This dynamic resets the exploitation horizon, allowing the agent to escape local optima while retaining the archive’s role as a scaffold for long-term learning.

### State Representation and Encoding

Go-Explore’s archive-based exploration strategy relies on a precise encoding of promising states to enable efficient rediscovery and reuse during red teaming of AI agents. The system represents states using a composite data structure that combines a **canonical state vector** with **action-conditioned metadata**, ensuring both uniqueness and contextual relevance. The canonical state vector captures the environment’s configuration as a fixed-length array of normalized features, including observable variables (e.g., agent position, object states, environmental flags) and derived metrics (e.g., reward gradients, uncertainty estimates). Each feature is discretized or quantized to a fixed precision, allowing for hashing while preserving discriminative power. The metadata supplements this vector with dynamic attributes such as the sequence of actions leading to the state, the agent’s internal policy parameters at the time, and observed outcomes (e.g., rewards, adversarial signals, or failure modes). This metadata is stored as a lightweight associative array linked to the state’s vector via a unique hash key, enabling rapid retrieval without reconstructing the full state history.

For graph-structured environments, Go-Explore employs a **state graph representation** where nodes are the canonical state vectors and edges encode the action-conditioned transitions. The graph uses adjacency lists with edge weights derived from the metadata, such as action costs or adversarial impact scores. To handle large state spaces, the system applies **locality-sensitive hashing (LSH)** to the canonical vectors, grouping similar states into buckets while preserving approximate nearest-neighbor relationships. This allows the archive to cluster related states without exact matches, improving exploration efficiency in high-dimensional spaces. The LSH parameters (e.g., hash family, bucket width) are tuned dynamically based on the state space’s sparsity and the red teaming task’s requirements.

The archive also implements a **priority queue** for promising states, where priority is determined by a composite score combining novelty, adversarial utility, and potential for further exploration. Novelty is measured using the state’s distance to its nearest neighbor in the archive, while adversarial utility is derived from the metadata’s outcome metrics (e.g., high reward variance or failure rates in prior evaluations). The priority queue ensures that the most critical states are revisited or expanded first, aligning with Go-Explore’s archive-driven exploration loop. To avoid redundancy, the system employs a **two-tier deduplication mechanism**: exact matches are filtered via hash collisions, and near-duplicates are pruned using the LSH buckets’ similarity thresholds. This dual approach balances storage efficiency with the preservation of diverse exploration paths.

**Reward** is computed as a composite signal combining intrinsic and extrinsic components. The coverage metric is recalculated periodically, with the clustering resolution adjusted based on the archive’s growth rate to maintain granularity.

Dynamic adjustment of these criteria occurs through a feedback loop where the archive’s composition influences the weighting of novelty, reward, and coverage. Conversely, if coverage gaps emerge in specific behavioral clusters, the clustering resolution is refined to prioritize states that fill these gaps. This adaptive mechanism ensures the archive remains balanced and maximally informative for red teaming throughout the exploration process.

The system applies unsupervised learning methods, such as k-means or DBSCAN, to embed states into a latent space derived from behavioral traces, environmental snapshots, or internal agent representations. Clustering parameters are dynamically adjusted to balance granularity and computational efficiency, ensuring that clusters neither fragment into noise nor collapse into overly broad groupings. Each cluster is annotated with centroids and representative exemplars, which serve as proxies for the entire group during retrieval operations. This allows the archive to prioritize exploration toward underrepresented or high-risk clusters, a mechanism critical for uncovering edge cases in adversarial testing.

During exploration, the Go-Explore algorithm queries the archive to identify the most promising underexplored states, using a scoring function that weights novelty, potential for further discovery, and relevance to red teaming objectives. The ANN index, typically implemented via hierarchical navigable small-world (HNSW) graphs or product quantization, prioritizes exploration by retrieving states that are semantically proximal but not identical. This balances exploitation—revisiting high-reward states—with exploration, as the red teaming agent can branch from states that share contextual features (e.g., similar failure modes in agent decision trees) but diverge in uncharted trajectories. The retrieval threshold for ANN queries is dynamically adjusted via a temperature parameter, where lower temperatures favor exploitation (narrowing to high-similarity states) and higher temperatures encourage broader exploration (accepting lower-similarity candidates). State prioritization during retrieval further refines the balance. Concurrency and memory locality are optimized through partitioned storage. Each shard maintains its own ANN index, enabling parallel retrieval and reducing contention during red teaming simulations. Sharding also localizes memory access patterns, as agents querying a specific shard (e.g., for prompt injection vulnerabilities) operate within a smaller, denser index, improving cache efficiency and reducing latency in high-throughput red teaming scenarios.

### Handling State Redundancy and Pruning

The archive in Go-Explore functions as a dynamic repository of promising states, but its utility degrades when redundancy inflates its size or dilutes its quality. Redundancy arises from cyclic revisits to states with identical or near-identical configurations, while low-promising states accumulate when their novelty scores decay below operational thresholds. To counteract this, the system employs a multi-layered pruning mechanism that operates at three distinct levels: raw state deduplication, similarity-based clustering, and decay-driven prioritization.

At the first level, exact duplicates are eliminated through cryptographic hashing of state representations. Each state is serialized into a fixed-length digest using SHA-256, where identical configurations produce identical hashes. This method ensures O(1) lookup time for duplicates, but its effectiveness depends on the fidelity of the serialization process. States with minor variations—such as those differing only in non-critical metadata—may evade detection unless the serialization protocol explicitly normalizes these fields. For example, if two states differ only in their timestamp or exploration step count but share identical environment configurations, their hashes will diverge, forcing the system to treat them as distinct. To mitigate this, the serialization layer can be augmented with a secondary hashing pass that ignores non-deterministic or irrelevant fields, though this introduces computational overhead.

The second layer addresses near-duplicates through locality-sensitive hashing (LSH), which maps similar states to the same hash bucket with high probability. Unlike exact hashing, LSH tolerates minor perturbations in state space by projecting states into a lower-dimensional space where proximity in the original space correlates with proximity in the hashed space. The choice of hash family (e.g., random projection, minhash) and bucketing parameters (e.g., number of hash functions, band size) directly influences the trade-off between false positives and false negatives. For instance, a conservative configuration with high similarity thresholds will cluster only highly congruent states, preserving diversity but risking redundant storage. Conversely, a lenient configuration may over-merge states, discarding potentially novel paths prematurely. Empirical tuning is required to align these parameters with the domain’s state space topology, often via cross-validation against a held-out set of known redundant states.

The third layer introduces decay-based pruning, where states are removed if their novelty scores fall below a dynamically adjusted threshold. Novelty is typically quantified as the inverse of visitation frequency or the inverse of the average distance to the k-nearest neighbors in state space. However, static thresholds prove brittle in environments with non-stationary dynamics, where promising states may temporarily lose relevance before regaining it. To address this, the system employs an exponential moving average (EMA) of novelty scores, weighted by a decay factor λ that reflects the environment’s volatility. States with EMA scores below a percentile-based cutoff (e.g., bottom 20% of the archive) are flagged for pruning. This approach ensures that states are not discarded permanently but are deprioritized, allowing them to re-enter the archive if their relevance resurges. The decay factor λ is itself adaptive, increasing in environments with high stochasticity to accelerate pruning of stale states.

Threshold-based pruning provides a complementary mechanism, where states are removed if their intrinsic reward (e.g., the reward signal used to guide exploration) drops below a predefined floor. This method is particularly effective in reward-sparse environments, where low-reward states are unlikely to yield future dividends. However, the floor must be calibrated to avoid premature culling of states that may lead to high-reward regions through longer trajectories. Reinforcement learning techniques, such as Q-learning or Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), can inform this calibration by identifying states that act as stepping stones to higher rewards, even if their immediate rewards are modest.

The interplay between these mechanisms requires careful orchestration.
